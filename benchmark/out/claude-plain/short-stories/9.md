The Sacrifice

Dmitri Volkov had lost every game he'd played in eleven years.

Not badly — never badly. He was, if you watched closely, the strongest player in the club by a wide margin. But somewhere in the middlegame, always, he found a way to offer up his queen, to walk his king into a mating net that hadn't existed a move before. The regulars at the Kingston Chess House had stopped commenting on it. New players sometimes crowed about their "win" over the quiet Russian, not noticing how their pieces had been herded into position like sheep through a gate.

Only Ilse, who ran the coffee counter, had ever asked him why.

"Because I was good enough to kill someone with it once," he told her, the only time he ever explained. "A tournament, a long time ago. My opponent had a heart condition nobody knew about. I was crushing him — mercilessly, the way you're taught to. He resigned, shook my hand, and collapsed in the hallway." He'd shrugged, stirring sugar into coffee he wouldn't drink. "I decided the board didn't need my ambition in it anymore."

So he lost. Politely, artfully, invisibly. It became, in its way, a kind of discipline — the hardest kind, since a strong player losing convincingly takes more calculation than winning.

Then, on a wet Tuesday in March, a boy named Sasha sat down across from him. Twelve years old, restless, already better than most adults in the room. He'd been brought by his mother, who worked two jobs and had spent her last free evening driving him here because a teacher said the boy had a gift worth nurturing.

Dmitri began, as always, to lose. He weakened his kingside. He offered the boy a knight for nothing.

Sasha didn't take it. He frowned at the board, found the actual best move instead, and refused the gift entirely — as if some instinct told him the position was being handed to him, and he wanted no part of a win he hadn't earned.

Dmitri felt something shift. He looked at the boy — not at the pieces, at the boy — and saw the same hunger he remembered having, once, before the hallway, before eleven years of small kindnesses disguised as bad chess.

He castled properly. He developed his bishop to the diagonal it belonged on. For the first time in over a decade, he began, quite simply, to play.

The game lasted two hours. Sasha fought with everything he had, and it wasn't enough, and when his king finally fell he didn't look devastated — he looked electrified, already replaying his errors, already hungry for the next board.

Dmitri sat back, his hands unsteady, astonished by how much it had cost him to want something again.

"Same time next week?" the boy asked.

"Yes," Dmitri said. "I think so."
