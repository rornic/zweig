**Static**

Elmer Voss had repaired radios since Eisenhower, and he trusted his ear more than any meter. So when the old Zenith on his bench started pulling in a signal at 4:47 every morning, he knew before he checked the schematics that something was wrong. The set didn't have a working antenna. He'd pulled it out himself, three days ago, to replace a cracked coil.

The first morning he thought he'd left something plugged in wrong. He traced the wiring, found nothing, and let it go.

The second morning, he sat down with coffee and listened. Under the hiss was a voice — a man's, calm, reading numbers. "Seven. Two. Four. One. Nine." Numbers stations existed, he knew that; had heard them decades back on shortwave, spies talking to spies in code. But those needed a transmitter somewhere in the world, and a receiver with an antenna to catch it. This Zenith had neither antenna nor, he confirmed with a multimeter, a live power connection. It was reading from a wall socket he had, out of habit, forgotten to plug in.

The third morning he brought his son-in-law, an electrical engineer, who laughed until he opened the back panel himself and found the voice still there, thin and patient, riding on nothing.

"It's picking up something in the wiring of the house," the son-in-law said, not believing it.

"House was built in 1958," Elmer said. "Same year as the set."

He began recording it on a cassette deck, numbers each morning, always at 4:47, always for exactly six minutes. He wrote them down. He looked for a pattern and told himself he wasn't looking for anything else — not his wife's birthday, not the serial number of the transport that took his brother to Korea, not any of the numbers a man carries into old age like stones in his pockets. But on the ninth morning the voice read a string he recognized without wanting to: his own service number, digit for digit, the one stenciled on a footlocker that had sat in his mother's attic for sixty years.

He unplugged the Zenith that night. In the morning, at 4:47, it played anyway.

He didn't tell his son-in-law that part. He sat instead with the coffee going cold, listening to a voice come from a box that wasn't wired to anything, reading him back pieces of a life he thought only he remembered, and found that he wasn't afraid so much as embarrassed — the way you feel when someone recites, gently, something true you never said out loud.

On the tenth morning, the numbers stopped. The voice said, once, clearly: "Received." Then only static, the ordinary kind, forever after.
