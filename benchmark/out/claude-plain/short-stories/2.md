The Backward Clock

Elias had built four hundred and eleven clocks in his life, and he could tell each one apart by ear alone, blindfolded, from the hallway. So when he woke at 3 a.m. to the sound of the walnut mantel clock in the front room, he knew immediately that something was wrong. The tick was the same. The tock had moved to where the tick should be.

He came downstairs in his nightshirt and watched the minute hand crawl, unmistakably, to the left.

It was the clock he'd made for Marta, thirty years ago, the year they married. After she died he hadn't been able to sell it or give it away, so it sat in the front room ticking forward like everything else, marking time he no longer much wanted marked.

He opened the case expecting a slipped gear, a reversed pawl, something with a simple mechanical name. Instead he found the escapement wheel turning the correct direction — and the hands turning the wrong one, as though the connection between motion and meaning had simply come loose somewhere in the works. He'd never seen anything like it. He tightened nothing, replaced nothing. He just watched it for a long while, the same way he used to watch Marta sleep, trying to catch the exact moment of a thing without disturbing it.

Three nights later he noticed the dust on the mantel had thinned. Then the water stain on the ceiling, from the winter the roof had leaked, was gone. Then, on a Tuesday that made no calendar sense, he found her reading glasses back on the side table, where they'd sat for years before he finally boxed them up.

He was an old man and he knew the difference between a wish and a fact, so he did not let himself believe anything he couldn't verify with his hands. But he stopped fixing the clock. He fed it, wound it gently at dusk instead of dawn, and did not ask it to explain itself.

He noticed, too, that he was sleeping more, eating less out of grief and more out of habit, and that some mornings he forgot, for a full and merciful minute, that she was gone at all.

He did not tell anyone about the clock. Not the widow next door, not his son, who called on Sundays and would have worried, gently, in the specific tone reserved for fathers living alone with old machines. Some things a man builds and some things build themselves back, and Elias had spent his whole life learning not to force a mechanism that wanted to run a particular way.

He simply sat with it some evenings, in the chair across from the mantel, and let it tick him somewhere he was in no hurry to arrive.
