The static in the headphones changed pitch at 2:14 a.m., and Aldous Marchetti had been keeping this light long enough to know the difference between weather and something else.

He'd inherited the radio from the previous keeper along with the lamp mechanism and a logbook full of gull counts. Nobody used it anymore. Ships had GPS, satellite phones, systems that didn't route through a lonely stone tower on the edge of the shipping lane. He kept it on out of habit, the way a man keeps a wedding ring after the divorce.

The voice came through in fragments, chewed by decades of interference. "...taking water... position four points off..." A pause, long enough that he almost logged it as noise and went back to his coffee. Then: "...Corvennes, requesting... do you copy..."

Corvennes. He knew the name the way everyone on this stretch of coast knew it, the way you know a scar. The freighter had gone down in a November gale in 1958, eleven years before Aldous was born, taking thirty-one men with her. There was a memorial plaque in the harbor town, green with verdigris, and an anniversary service every year that fewer people attended.

He should have written it off as a fluke, some ghost frequency bouncing off the ionosphere, a fragment of an old broadcast finally arriving after seventy years of wandering. He'd read about that happening. Radio waves didn't just vanish; sometimes they came back around.

But he answered anyway. Pressed the transmit key and gave his position, his light's signature flash, told them where the rocks were.

Silence for a full minute. Then, weaker: "...copy... light in sight... thank you..."

Nothing after that. He sat with the headphones still on, hearing only the ordinary hiss of the empty channel, until the sky over the water went from black to the color of a fresh bruise and the automatic shutoff dimmed the lamp for the day.

He didn't put it in the log. There was no column for it, no honest way to write down what he wasn't sure had happened. But that evening, before he lit the lamp, he walked down to check the rocks below the tower, the ones the charts still marked as a hazard, the ones the Corvennes was presumed to have never reached.

Nothing there. No wreckage, no confirmation, nothing but the tide doing what it always did. Just a man alone with a light and a stretch of dark water that had, for one night, apparently needed it.

He kept the radio on after that. Some habits you don't examine too closely. You just leave the door open, in case someone is still trying to find their way home.
